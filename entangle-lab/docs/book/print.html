Entangle Lab Field Guide

Welcome to the lab

Two observers. One shared quantum state.

Entangle Lab is a small interactive experiment about one of the clearest ways to see how quantum predictions differ from local classical ones. Alice and Bob each measure one particle from a shared singlet state. Their individual results are random. When they compare many results, the correlations depend on the angle between their measurement directions.

The field guide introduces the pieces in order. You can read it without a physics background beyond basic algebra. Each chapter connects one idea to a control or result in the simulator.

Start with the experiment. Open the project page, leave Alice at 0° and Bob at 45°, then move either angle. The bars show the probabilities predicted by the ideal model. Click Measure one pair to draw one random outcome.

What you will discover

  • A qubit can have a state that is not a definite classical bit.
  • A pair of qubits can share a state that cannot be described as two independent states.
  • Measurement outcomes are random locally, while their joint statistics can be strongly correlated.
  • The CHSH inequality gives a numerical test of one family of local hidden-variable models.
  • Finite data fluctuates, and a browser simulation is not a laboratory experiment.

A note on the model

The simulator samples the quantum probability distribution for an ideal two-spin singlet. It does not generate physical particles or prove a theorem from experimental data. The model limits chapter explains what those distinctions mean.

This field guide covers the singlet and Bell-test teaching playground. The repository's companion Entanglement Trap project takes the next step into quantum channel discrimination; it has a separate field guide.

Start here

Run the page

From the repository root, start a local server:

python -m http.server 8000

Open http://localhost:8000/entangle-lab/. Entangle Lab uses plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. The simulation needs no installation and makes no network requests; optional typefaces are fetched from Google Fonts, with system fallbacks.

Your first experiment

  1. Find The experiment. Alice begins at 0°, Bob at 45°.
  2. Read the expected correlation and the two joint-outcome probabilities.
  3. Click Measure one pair a few times. Individual outcomes vary.
  4. Set both axes to 0°. The singlet predicts opposite results with probability 1 in the ideal model.
  5. Find The Bell test and click Run Bell test. The four default settings are chosen to produce a CHSH value near the quantum maximum when there are enough samples.

Your measured number will not be exactly the theoretical value. The app draws a finite random sample, so a different run gives a slightly different result.

A qubit in two minutes

A classical bit stores either 0 or 1. A qubit is a two-level quantum system. Its pure state can be written

$$ |\psi\rangle = \alpha |0\rangle + \beta |1\rangle, \qquad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1. $$

The complex numbers α and β are amplitudes. In a measurement using the 0/1 basis, the Born rule gives probability |α|² for 0 and |β|² for 1. A superposition is not just a hidden classical choice between the two values; amplitudes can interfere when different operations are applied.

Two qubits

Two qubits have four basis states: |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, and |11⟩. A general pure state is

$$ |\Psi\rangle = c_{00}|00\rangle + c_{01}|01\rangle + c_{10}|10\rangle + c_{11}|11\rangle. $$

If the state can be factored into one state for each qubit, |Ψ⟩ = |u⟩ ⊗ |v⟩, the pair is separable. If it cannot be factored that way, it is entangled.

Check your intuition

The state (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 is entangled. Its measurement results are either 00 or 11, each with probability 1/2. It is not a product state: a product with a 50/50 result on the first qubit and a 50/50 result on the second would also assign probability to 01 and 10.

Entangle Lab uses a different entangled state, the singlet, because its correlations have a simple rotational form.

The singlet state

Entangle Lab models a pair of spin-½ systems in the singlet state:

$$ |\psi^-\rangle = \frac{|01\rangle - |10\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}. $$

The minus sign is a relative phase. It matters for how the state behaves under rotations, even though measuring both particles in the computational basis gives 01 or 10, each with probability 1/2.

What “shared” means

This is one joint state, not two particles carrying separate pure states. Neither particle alone has a definite spin along a chosen axis. If Alice and Bob measure along the same axis, their outcomes are always opposite in the ideal model. If their axes differ, the chance of agreement changes smoothly with the angle.

For measurement directions a and b in one plane, the ideal singlet correlation is

$$ E(a,b) = \langle A B \rangle = -\cos(\theta_a - \theta_b). $$

Here each measurement result A and B is represented by +1 or −1, and E is the average of the product A × B over many identically prepared pairs. At equal angles, E = −1: outcomes are opposite. At a right angle, E = 0: they are uncorrelated. At opposite directions, E = +1: the spin results agree.

The simulator takes the angle difference in degrees and evaluates this equation directly. It does not evolve a wavefunction through quantum gates.

Measurement and probability

When Alice chooses a direction θ_A and Bob chooses θ_B, each receives one result, +1 or −1. The joint probabilities for the singlet are

$$ P(A=a, B=b) = \frac{1 + ab E(\theta_A,\theta_B)}{4}, \qquad a,b \in {-1,+1}. $$

Using E = −cos(θ_A − θ_B), the probability that the two results are the same is

$$ P(\text{same}) = \frac{1+E}{2} = \frac{1-\cos(\theta_A-\theta_B)}{2}. $$

The probability that they are opposite is 1 − P(same). At 0° separation, same outcomes have probability zero. At 90°, both categories have probability 1/2.

Why one result is not the result

Every single measurement is random. Alice alone sees +1 about half the time and −1 about half the time, regardless of Bob's setting. The useful pattern appears in the joint data from many pairs.

This is why the app separates the expected distribution from a sampled result. The probability bars show the model's prediction. Measure one pair draws one example from that distribution. Repeating the click does not guarantee a balanced short run.

Sampling rule used by the app

The code first draws Alice's result with equal probability for +1 and −1. It then draws Bob's result with

$$ P(B=+1\mid A=a) = \frac{1+aE}{2}. $$

This gives the joint probabilities above while keeping each local result unbiased. The same rule is applied independently to each simulated pair.

The CHSH Bell test

The CHSH test uses two possible measurement settings for Alice (a₀, a₁) and two for Bob (b₀, b₁). A setting pair is used on many separately prepared pairs. For each pair of settings, estimate the correlation E by averaging the products of the two outcomes.

One common CHSH combination is

$$ S = E(a_0,b_0) + E(a_0,b_1) + E(a_1,b_0) - E(a_1,b_1). $$

For local hidden-variable models that satisfy the usual assumptions of the Bell test, |S| ≤ 2. Quantum theory predicts that |S| can reach 2√2, but not exceed it. The CHSH form was proposed as an experimentally testable version of Bell's argument by Clauser, Horne, Shimony, and Holt in 1969.

Why local models stop at 2

For a deterministic local model, each hidden state assigns an answer A₀, A₁, B₀, B₁ ∈ {−1,+1} to the four possible settings. Its CHSH expression for that hidden state is

$$ C = A_0B_0 + A_0B_1 + A_1B_0 - A_1B_1 = A_0(B_0+B_1) + A_1(B_0-B_1). $$

If B₀ and B₁ agree, the first parenthesis is ±2 and the second is 0. If they disagree, the first is 0 and the second is ±2. Therefore C is always +2 or −2. Averaging over any distribution of hidden states gives |S| ≤ 2. Stochastic local models can be represented as mixtures of deterministic responses, so they obey the same bound.

Settings that expose the difference

For the singlet, E(a,b) = −cos(a−b). Choose Alice's angles a₀ = 0°, a₁ = 90°, and Bob's b₀ = 45°, b₁ = −45°. The four ideal correlations combine to

$$ S = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = -2\sqrt{2}. $$

The app displays |S|, so the ideal magnitude is 2√2 ≈ 2.828. In a run, the four values are estimated from random samples and the result fluctuates around this value. Increase the number of pairs to see the estimates usually settle closer to the prediction.

What a violation tells us

A measured violation rules out the family of local hidden-variable explanations under the experiment's assumptions. It does not show that Alice can send a faster-than-light message. Her local outcomes remain random, and changing Bob's measurement choice does not change Alice's local outcome statistics.

Real experiments must address how settings are chosen, how events are detected, and whether the observations are statistically independent. The browser simulation assumes an ideal source and measurement process, so it illustrates the prediction rather than closing those experimental loopholes.

How the simulator works

The app is deliberately small: index.html provides the controls, styles.css draws the interface, and app.js implements the probability model. There is no app server, account, or backend.

One pair

The app converts angles from degrees to radians and evaluates

E = −cos(θA − θB)
P(same) = (1 + E) / 2
P(opposite) = 1 − P(same)

When you click Measure one pair, JavaScript samples A = +1 or −1 with equal probability. It then samples Bob's result conditionally so that P(B = +1 | A = a) = (1 + aE) / 2. This reproduces the singlet joint distribution.

Four setting pairs

The Bell test calls the same sampling rule for each of the four setting pairs. For each pair, it estimates the correlation as

Ê = (number of equal outcomes − number of different outcomes) / number of pairs

That is equivalent to averaging A × B, because equal outcomes contribute +1 and different outcomes contribute −1. The app combines the four estimates using the CHSH formula and displays the magnitude.

The random-number generator is JavaScript's Math.random(). It is suitable for a classroom visualization, not security or precision experimental work. Separate runs give different finite samples.

Try these settings

| Alice | Bob | Ideal |S| | What to notice | | --- | --- | ---: | --- | | 0°, 90° | 45°, −45° | 2√2 | The standard maximum-violation settings | | 0°, 90° | 0°, 90° | 2 | Correlations combine to the classical bound | | 0°, 0° | 0°, 0° | 2 | Aligned singlet results are always opposite |

These are theoretical values. A finite simulated run only estimates them.

What the model leaves out

Entangle Lab is an ideal mathematical simulator. It is useful for exploring the relation between measurement angles, probabilities, correlations, and the CHSH statistic. It leaves out important parts of real quantum experiments.

No physical device

The app computes the ideal singlet distribution directly. It does not prepare particles, run a quantum circuit on hardware, or record detector events. The display is a visualization of a model.

No noise or experimental loopholes

Real experiments must account for imperfect state preparation, measurement error, loss, detector efficiency, finite timing, and the independence of random setting choices. The app assumes perfect preparation and measurement, and it samples setting pairs separately by construction. It cannot establish that a laboratory experiment is loophole-free.

Finite-sample variation

Each run uses pseudorandom samples and a finite number of pairs per setting. An estimated |S| may be above or below both 2 and the quantum prediction. Such a result is sampling variation in the demo, not evidence that the underlying mathematical bounds changed.

The slider changes the number of pairs for each of the four setting combinations. It is not a single total spread across all four combinations.

Scope of the conclusion

The CHSH bound applies to local hidden-variable models with the assumptions used in deriving the inequality. A violation constrains those models. It does not permit faster-than-light communication, and it does not select a unique interpretation of quantum mechanics.

When presenting the project, call it an interactive simulation of the ideal singlet CHSH prediction. Do not describe it as a physical Bell test or as experimental evidence collected by the project.

Glossary and sources

Glossary

Amplitude: A complex number in a quantum state. Its squared magnitude contributes to a measurement probability.

Bell state: One of four maximally entangled two-qubit states. The singlet is one of them.

Born rule: The rule that maps quantum amplitudes to probabilities for measurement outcomes.

Correlation, E: The average product of paired outcomes A × B. For outcomes ±1, it ranges from −1 to +1.

Entangled state: A joint state that cannot be written as a product of separate states for each subsystem.

Local hidden-variable model: A class of models in which outcomes depend on shared past variables and each observer's local setting, with no influence from the distant setting.

Measurement setting: The direction or configuration an observer chooses for a measurement.

Singlet: The two-spin state (|01⟩ − |10⟩)/√2, with perfect anti-correlation for aligned spin measurements.
